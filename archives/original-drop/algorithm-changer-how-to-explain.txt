Yes — I think you’ve stumbled onto something bigger than a search product. The weak point you identified is real: if the user does not keep learning, integrating, and updating their internal model, they will gradually collapse back into old query habits and the system’s gains will decay. Research on self-regulated learning and metacognition supports the idea that durable improvement depends on planning, monitoring, feedback, and reflection rather than passive exposure alone, while work on “desirable difficulties” suggests that learning sticks better when effortful retrieval, spacing, and variation are built into the process.

That means your system may have two products hidden inside one: a research OS and a learning OS. The first improves answer paths; the second upgrades the person using those paths by turning search behavior into a feedback-rich learning loop.

The deeper idea
Your insight is that search quality degrades when the user’s internal model stays static. If curiosity fades, new knowledge is not assimilated, and query behavior remains unchanged, the person reverts to the same intellectual posture and the system loses altitude.

So the real product may not be “better search” alone. It may be a system that continuously measures, strengthens, and refreshes a user’s research posture: what they now understand, how deeply they understand it, whether they can retrieve it, and whether their behavior shows genuine progression rather than repeated browsing.

Proof of interest
The “proof of interest” concept is promising, but it must be designed carefully. Dwell time by itself is weak and often misleading; a person can linger because they are confused, distracted, or overwhelmed, while a strong learner may extract value quickly and move on. Research on metacognition and self-regulated learning points more toward combining behavior, reflection, retrieval, and follow-through than relying on time alone.

A stronger proof-of-interest model would combine:

Dwell time adjusted for content type and difficulty.

Return frequency to a topic over spaced intervals.

Query evolution, meaning whether questions become more precise and conceptually advanced.

Retrieval evidence, meaning whether the person can summarize or apply what was learned.

Transfer evidence, meaning whether they use the new concept in adjacent contexts.

Source progression, meaning whether they move toward stronger source classes over time.

That would be much more than “the user stayed on page for 7 minutes.” It becomes a behavioral signal of active assimilation.

A new teaching model
Yes — this could become a new teaching model, especially for adults, operators, and self-directed learners. Instead of treating learning as content delivery, the system would treat learning as trajectory correction: detect the learner’s current posture, expose them to stronger materials, require some retrieval or synthesis, observe whether their next questions improve, and then adapt again. Research on retrieval practice and feedback suggests that this kind of cycle improves long-term retention and transfer better than passive rereading or repeated exposure.

In practical terms, the system could teach by quietly shaping behavior:

It asks for a short restatement before giving the next layer.

It notices when the user keeps circling the same shallow question.

It inserts contrast cases and better source material.

It schedules spaced revisits.

It rewards conceptual advancement, not just consumption.

That starts to look less like search and more like an adaptive intellectual trainer.

The grading idea
A “grade” is useful only if it measures growth and is hard to game. If it becomes a vanity score, users will optimize for optics rather than learning.

A better approach is a trajectory score with multiple dimensions:

Curiosity: how often the user opens new but relevant terrain.

Assimilation: whether new material changes future queries and summaries.

Retention: whether prior ideas can be recalled later.

Transfer: whether knowledge shows up in adjacent domains.

Discernment: whether source choices improve over time.

Independence: whether the user increasingly needs less scaffolding for good questions.

That score would be more defensible because it tracks movement, not ego.

Product direction
This suggests a three-layer product architecture:

Research layer: better retrieval, reranking, memory, and briefing.

Learning layer: retrieval prompts, spaced revisits, metacognitive checks, and concept progression.

Annealing layer: detect failure, adjust system behavior, and measure whether the user’s behavior is actually improving.

If built well, the product’s claim becomes much stronger:

It does not just help you find better answers. It helps you become the kind of person who can keep finding better answers.

That is a much bigger category.

Caution
There is one major risk: once you move from “search improvement” to “teaching humans,” you are entering a more sensitive territory. Claims about intelligence, learning outcomes, or behavioral grading must be conservative, evidence-based, and ethically framed. Dwell-time surveillance or overconfident “intelligence scores” could make the product feel manipulative rather than empowering.

The safest framing is:

The system measures research behaviors that correlate with deeper learning and better discovery, then uses those signals to improve both the workflow and the learner’s trajectory.

That keeps the focus on behavior and growth rather than labeling the person.
